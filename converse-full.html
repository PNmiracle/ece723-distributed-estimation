II-B · Statistics

Score and Fisher information

The score: slope of the log-likelihood
Each sample gives a log-likelihood curve; its slope at the true θ is the score \(\xi_\theta(x)\). Slopes average to zero (15); their spread is \(I_X\).
\[ \class{tS}{\xi_\theta(x)}:=\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x),\qquad \class{tI}{I_X(\theta)}:=\mathbb E_\theta\big[\xi_\theta(X)\xi_\theta(X)^\top\big] \]
\[ \mathbb E_\theta\|\class{tS}{\xi_\theta(X)}\|^2\lt \infty,\qquad\mathbb E_\theta\,\class{tS}{\xi_\theta(X)}=0 \]
\[ \int\class{tS}{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}\,\nu(dx)=\nabla_\theta\int p_\theta(x)\,\nu(dx)=\nabla_\theta 1=0 \]
\[ p_\theta^T(t)=\int \class{tK}{K(t\mid z)}\,\class{tP}{\prod_{a=1}^q p_\theta(x_a)}\,\nu^{\otimes q}(dz) \]
\[ \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^T(T)=\mathbb E_\theta\Big[\class{tS}{\sum_{a=1}^q\xi_\theta(X_a)}\ \Big|\ T\Big] \]
The converse measures information with Fisher information. Here are the definitions, and the one identity the whole proof keeps using: a message's score is an average of raw scores.
II-B · Statistics

The score condition: the only quantitative assumption

Score condition (19) in one direction
Below the bound everywhere ⇒ below it in curvature at \(t=0\) ⇒ \(I_X\preceq vI_d\).
\[ \log\mathbb E_\theta\exp\!\big(\class{tU}{u}^\top\xi_\theta(X)\big)\le\class{tV}{\frac{v\|u\|^2}{2(1-b\|u\|)}},\qquad b\|u\|\lt 1 \]
\[ \log\mathbb E_\theta\exp\big(\class{tU}{t\,a^\top}\xi_\theta(X)\big)\le\class{tV}{\frac{vt^2}{2(1-b|t|)}} \]
\[ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log\mathbb E_\theta e^{ta^\top\xi_\theta(X)}\Big|_{t=0}=\class{tI}{a^\top I_X(\theta)a}\le \class{tV}{v} \]
\[ \class{tI}{I_X(\theta)\preceq vI_d},\qquad \mathrm{tr}\,I_X(\theta)\le dv \]
\[ A_q:=\sum_{a=1}^q\xi_\theta(X_a),\qquad \log\mathbb E_\theta e^{u^\top A_q}\le \frac{\class{tV}{qv}\|u\|^2}{2(1-b\|u\|)} \]
The converse uses exactly one quantitative assumption on the model: a Bernstein-type bound on the score's moment generating function. It is used three ways later; here we get the first, a cap on Fisher information.

III-A · Tools

Score identity: a message averages the raw score

A message averages the input scores
Equally likely inputs are routed to messages; each message’s score is the average input score of its group (75).
\[ \class{tP}{p_\theta^T(t)}=\int K(t\mid z)\,p_\theta^Z(z)\,\nu_Z(dz) \]
\[ \nabla_\theta \class{tP}{p_\theta^T(t)}=\int K(t\mid z)\,p_\theta^Z(z)\,\class{tS}{\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^Z(z)}\,\nu_Z(dz) \]
\[ \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^T(T)=\mathbb E_\theta\big[\class{tS}{\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^Z(Z)}\mid T\big] \]
\[ \class{tS}{\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^Z(Z)}=\sum_{a=1}^q\xi_\theta(X_a),\qquad \class{tJ}{J_Z(\theta)=q\,I_X(\theta)} \]
Section Three-A starts with the score identity, now for any input Z, not only i.i.d. blocks.
III-A · Tools

Data processing: a message carries no more information

Averaging shrinks information
The message score is a conditional mean of the raw score; by Jensen its second moment is smaller: \(J_T\preceq J_Z\).
\[ \class{tZ}{S_Z}:=\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^Z(Z),\qquad \class{tT}{S_T}:=\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta^T(T) \]
\[ \class{tA}{a^\top J_T a}=\mathbb E\big(\class{tT}{\mathbb E[a^\top S_Z\mid T]}\big)^2\le\mathbb E(a^\top \class{tZ}{S_Z})^2=\class{tB}{a^\top J_Z a} \]
\[ \class{tA}{J_T(\theta)}\preceq \class{tB}{J_Z(\theta)} \]
With the score identity, data processing is one line of Jensen.
III-A · Tools

Fisher chain rule: observe G, then T

Information of a pair = first + average extra
Because the cross term vanishes, the two pieces add exactly (83)–(84). Theorem 2 uses this with G = shared samples.
\[ \class{tJ}{S_{G,T}}=\class{tG}{S_G}+\class{tC}{S_{T\mid G}} \]
\[ \mathbb E_\theta[\class{tC}{S_{T\mid G}}\mid G]=0 \]
\[ \mathbb E_\theta[\class{tG}{S_G}\,\class{tC}{S_{T\mid G}^\top}]=\mathbb E_\theta\big[S_G\,\mathbb E_\theta[S_{T\mid G}^\top\mid G]\big]=0 \]
\[ \class{tJ}{J_{G,T}}=\class{tG}{J_G}+\class{tC}{\mathbb E_{G}J_{T\mid G}(\theta;G)} \]
\[ \class{tJ}{\mathrm{tr}\,J_{G,T}(\theta)}=\class{tG}{\mathrm{tr}\,J_G(\theta)}+\class{tC}{\mathbb E_G\,\mathrm{tr}\,J_{T\mid G}(\theta;G)} \]
Theorem Two will reveal the shared samples G and then look at the transcript. The chain rule tells us how information adds.
III-A · Tools

A smooth prior with explicit constants

Prior on one coordinate: \(w(t)=2\sin^2(\pi t)\)
Smooth, zero at both ends: van Trees can integrate by parts.
\[ \pi(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^d\class{tW}{2\sin^2(\pi\theta_j)},\qquad \class{tH}{h(\pi)=d(\ln 2-1)},\qquad \class{tJ}{J_\pi=4\pi^2d} \]
\[ \class{tJ}{J_\pi}:=\int\|\nabla_\theta\log\pi(\theta)\|^2\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta \]
\[ \frac d{dt}\log \class{tW}{w(t)}=\class{tC}{2\pi\cot(\pi t)},\qquad w(t)=2\sin^2(\pi t) \]
\[ \int_0^1\big(\class{tC}{2\pi\cot(\pi t)}\big)^2w(t)\,dt=8\pi^2\int_0^1\cos^2(\pi t)\,dt=\class{tJ}{4\pi^2} \]
\[ \class{tE}{\mathbb E_w\ln\sin^2(\pi\Theta_1)}=\tfrac4\pi F'(2)=1-2\ln2 \]
\[ h(w)=-\ln2-\class{tE}{\mathbb E_w\ln\sin^2(\pi\Theta_1)}=\class{tH}{\ln2-1} \]
To pass from Fisher information to a risk floor we average over a prior. The paper picks a product of squared sines; we need its Fisher information and its entropy.
III-A · Tools

van Trees: information caps accuracy on average

van Trees: less information ⇒ higher floor
Any estimator’s Bayes risk sits on or above the curve. Lower the information, raise the floor.
\[ \class{tD}{D}:=\mathbb E\|\widehat\theta(Y)-\Theta\|^2 \]
\[ \class{tQ}{Q(\theta,y)}:=\nabla_\theta\log\big(\pi(\theta)p_\theta^Y(y)\big)=\nabla\log\pi(\theta)+\nabla\log p_\theta^Y(y) \]
\[ \mathbb E\big[(\widehat\theta_j-\Theta_j)\,\class{tQ}{Q_j}\big]=1 \]
\[ d=\mathbb E\langle\widehat\theta-\Theta,\class{tQ}{Q}\rangle\le\sqrt{\class{tD}{\mathbb E\|\widehat\theta-\Theta\|^2}}\sqrt{\class{tI}{\mathbb E\|Q\|^2}} \]
\[ \class{tI}{\mathbb E\|Q\|^2}=J_\pi+\mathbb E_{\Theta\sim\pi}\mathrm{tr}\,J_Y(\Theta) \]
\[ D\ \ge\ \frac{d^2}{\class{tI}{\mathbb E_{\Theta\sim\pi}\mathrm{tr}\,J_Y(\Theta)+J_\pi}} \]
\[ \sup_{\theta}\mathbb E_\theta\|\widehat\theta-\theta\|^2\ \ge\ \int\mathbb E_\theta\|\widehat\theta-\theta\|^2\pi(\theta)\,d\theta=\class{tD}{D} \]
van Trees is the Bayesian Cramér–Rao bound. From now on, lower-bounding error means upper-bounding information.
III-A · Floors 1–2

Sample-size floors: d/N and d/(nm)

Distinct samples anyone sees
The transcript can only use visible samples, each worth ≤ dv. So the floor is at least \(d/N\) and \(d/(nm)\) (107).
\[ \class{tP}{\mathbb P\Big(a\notin\bigcup_{i=1}^nS_i\Big)=(1-m/N)^n} \]
\[ \class{tU}{\mathbb EU}=N\big[1-\class{tP}{(1-m/N)^n}\big]\le\min\{N,nm\} \]
\[ \class{tT}{\mathrm{tr}\,J_{T\mid W=w}(\theta)}\le \class{tI}{dv}\,U(\mathcal S) \]
\[ \mathrm{tr}\,J_{T,W}(\theta)=\mathbb E_W\class{tT}{\mathrm{tr}\,J_{T\mid W}}\le dv\,\class{tU}{\mathbb EU} \]
\[ R_{\mathrm{BB}}\ \ge\ \frac{d}{v\,\class{tU}{\mathbb EU}+4\pi^2}\ \gtrsim\ d\max\{N^{-1},(nm)^{-1}\} \]
The first two floors ignore the bit budget. The transcript is a function of at most U distinct samples, so it cannot carry more than d v U.
III-A · Floor 3

The bit floor: information needs bits

The bit floor decays like 2^(−2B/d)
Small error needs information (green) that B bits can carry only up to \(B\ln2\) (red). Solving for D gives the exponential floor.
\[ \class{tY}{D_y}:=\mathbb E\big[\|\Theta-\widehat\theta(y)\|^2\mid Y=y\big],\qquad \mathbb E_YD_Y=\class{tD}{D} \]
\[ h(\Theta\mid Y=y)=h(\Theta-\widehat\theta(y)\mid Y=y)\le\class{tG}{\frac d2\ln\Big(\frac{2\pi e\,\class{tY}{D_y}}d\Big)} \]
\[ h(\Theta\mid Y)\le\frac d2\mathbb E_Y\ln\Big(\frac{2\pi eD_Y}d\Big)\le\class{tG}{\frac d2\ln\Big(\frac{2\pi eD}d\Big)} \]
\[ \class{tI}{I(\Theta;T,W)}\ge \class{tH}{h(\pi)}-\class{tG}{\frac d2\ln(2\pi eD/d)} \]
\[ \class{tI}{I(\Theta;T,W)}=I(\Theta;T\mid W) \]
\[ I(\Theta;T\mid W)\le H(T\mid W)\le \class{tB}{B\ln2} \]
\[ D\ \ge\ \frac{d}{2\pi e}\exp\Big(\frac{2\,\class{tH}{h(\pi)}}{d}\Big)\,\class{tB}{2^{-2B/d}} \]
\[ D\ \ge\ \class{tC}{\frac{2d}{\pi e^3}}\,\class{tB}{2^{-2B/d}} \]
The third floor uses entropy instead of Fisher information. A small error means the transcript must locate theta, and B bits can only say so much.

III-B · Concentration

Block score and its Bernstein bound

A block of q samples: variance grows like qv
Independent scores add, so their log-MGF bounds add: \(v\to qv\), while the tail constant \(b\) stays.
\[ \class{tA}{A_q}:=\sum_{a=1}^{q}\xi_\theta(X_a) \]
\[ \log\mathbb E_\theta e^{u^\top \class{tA}{A_q}}=\sum_{a=1}^q\log\mathbb E_\theta e^{u^\top\xi_\theta(X_a)}\le\class{tV}{\frac{vq\|u\|^2}{2(1-b\|u\|)}} \]
Section Three-B studies one machine's block of q samples and asks: how far can the average score move if we only look at outcomes in some event E?
III-B · Lemma 1

Lemma 1: a rare event can shift the score only a little

Conditioning on a rare event shifts the mean
Rarer event (smaller \(p\)) ⇒ larger possible shift, but only like \(\sqrt{\ln(1/p)}\).
\[ \|\mathbb E_\theta[A_q\mid E]\|\ \le\ \class{tG}{\sqrt{2vq\ln(1/p)}}+\class{tB}{b\ln(1/p)} \]
\[ \|\mathbb E_\theta[A_q\mid E]\|^2\ \le\ \class{tG}{4vq\ln(1/p)}+\class{tB}{2b^2\ln^2(1/p)} \]
\[ \|\mathbb E_\theta[A_q\mid E]\|^2\ \le\ \class{tG}{2vq\ln(1/p)}\qquad(b=0) \]
Lemma One is the heart of Three-B. Think of E as the event that a machine sends a particular message. How much can learning E move the average score?
III-B · Lemma 1

Proof of Lemma 1: tilt, condition, optimize

Choosing the tilt λ
Large λ makes the surprisal cost \(\ell/\lambda\) small but the tail cost large. \(\lambda_*\) balances them (131).
\[ \class{tL}{\ell}:=\ln(1/p),\qquad \class{tM}{\mu_E}:=\mathbb E_\theta[A_q\mid E] \]
\[ \exp(\lambda\|\class{tM}{\mu_E}\|)\le\mathbb E_\theta\big[e^{\lambda e^\top A_q}\mid E\big],\qquad e:=\mu_E/\|\mu_E\| \]
\[ \mathbb E_\theta[e^{\lambda e^\top A_q}\mid E]=\frac{\mathbb E_\theta[e^{\lambda e^\top A_q}\mathbf 1_E]}{\class{tP}{p}}\le\frac{\mathbb E_\theta e^{\lambda e^\top A_q}}{\class{tP}{p}} \]
\[ \lambda\|\mu_E\|\le\class{tL}{\ell}+\class{tV}{\frac{vq\lambda^2}{2(1-b\lambda)}},\qquad b\lambda\lt 1 \]
\[ \|\mu_E\|\le\class{tL}{\frac\ell\lambda}+\class{tV}{\frac{vq\lambda}{2(1-b\lambda)}} \]
\[ \eta:=\sqrt{\frac{2\class{tL}{\ell}}{vq}},\qquad \lambda_*:=\frac{\eta}{1+b\eta} \]
\[ \class{tL}{\frac\ell{\lambda_*}}=\sqrt{\frac{vq\ell}2}+b\ell,\qquad \class{tV}{\frac{vq\lambda_*}{2(1-b\lambda_*)}}=\sqrt{\frac{vq\ell}2} \]
\[ \|\mu_E\|^2\le\big(\class{tS}{\sqrt{2vq\ell}}+\class{tB}{b\ell}\big)^2\le 4vq\ell+2b^2\ell^2 \]
The proof has three moves: look along the shift direction, pay ln one over p for conditioning, and choose the tilt optimally.
III-B · Averaging

Average Lemma 1 over all messages

Average surprisal is entropy
Lemma 1 charges each message its surprisal; averaging with weights \(p(t)\) gives \(H(T)\) (136).
\[ \mathbb E_\theta\|\mathbb E_\theta[A_q\mid T]\|^2\le\class{tH}{4vq\,H_\theta(T)}+\class{tK}{2b^2\,\mathbb E_\theta\ln^2\frac1{p_\theta(T)}} \]
\[ \mathbb E_\theta\|\mathbb E_\theta[A_q\mid T]\|^2\le\class{tH}{2vq\,H_\theta(T)}\qquad(b=0) \]
Now apply Lemma One to every event T equals t, with p equal to the probability of that message, and average over t.
III-B · Lemma 2

Lemma 2: at most 2^B messages ⇒ squared surprisal ≤ K_B

Tail of the surprisal \(L_T\)
At most \(2^B\) messages: few can be very surprising.
\[ \class{tL}{L_T}:=\ln\frac1{p_T(T)} \]
\[ \mathbb P(\class{tL}{L_T}\gt x)=\sum_{t:\,p_T(t)\lt e^{-x}}p_T(t)\le \class{tE}{2^Be^{-x}} \]
\[ \class{tP}{\mathbb P(L_T\gt x)}\le\min\{1,\,\class{tE}{2^Be^{-x}}\} \]
\[ \mathbb E L_T^2=\int_0^\infty 2x\,\class{tP}{\mathbb P(L_T\gt x)}\,dx \]
\[ \mathbb E L_T^2\le\int_0^a2x\,dx+\class{tI}{\int_a^\infty2xe^{a-x}\,dx}=\class{tK}{a^2+2a+2},\qquad a=B\ln2 \]
\[ \mathbb E\ln^2\frac1{p_T(T)}\le K_B,\qquad K_B:=\class{tK}{(B\ln2)^2+2B\ln2+2} \]
Note \(K_B\le C_K(B+1)^2\): the squared-surprisal term grows only like \(B^2\). (145): the same holds conditionally on \(W\).
Lemma Two controls the quadratic term. It only uses that there are at most two to the B possible transcripts.
